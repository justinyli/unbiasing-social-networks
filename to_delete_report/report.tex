\section{Introduction and Motivation}

Surveys of "hard-to-reach" populations face an obstacle as these populations of interest, which often face stigma, marginalization, or are otherwise rare, lack manageable sampling frames, and their members are too sparsely distributed in the general population to be reached efficiently through conventional survey design. New York City's jazz musicians constitute one such population. Introduced by Heckathorn (1997) to study this population, Respondent-Driven Sampling (RDS) and its extensions by Salganik and Heckathorn (2004), Heckathorn (2002, 2007), and Volz and Heckathorn (2008), address this obstacle, as Gile and Handcock (2010) frame, in two main ways. First, RDS employs a chain-referral design by beginning from a convenience sample of "seed" respondents; each participant is given a small, fixed number of coupons to distribute to peers within the target population, who in turn recruit further participants, thus exploiting the social network of a given population to draw respondents. Second, by modeling the recruitment process as a Markov chain, where each participant recruits the next participant in their network at random, sufficient waves of recruitment will eventually reduce "seed bias" from the convenience sample, so RDS methods allow populations-level characteristics to be estimated. 

Volz and Heckathorn (2008) thus establish an unbiased, generalized Horowitz-Thompson estimator that encodes these assumptions, whereby for a given characteristic of the population $y$:
$$\hat{y} := \sum_{i\in V_S} \frac{y_i/d(i)}{\sum_{j\in V_S}1/d(j)}$$
This estimator assumes that the random walk will converge to a stationary distribution, proportional to each node's degree. Yet this estimator assumes that the Markov chain has run long enough to be near its stationary distribution to have properly mixed. Next, the estimator requires that respondents accurately report their network degree. Lastly, we address a more structural source of bias arising from homophily, or the tendency of individuals to recruit disproportionately from within their own sub-communities. With this estimator, respondents are assumed to randomly recommend a friend, although this is rarely the case. When recruitment is not well-mixed across subcommunities, the collected RDS sample will be biased toward starting seed bias. This problem is particularly pronounced when there are community bottlenecks. We build on research conducted by Zhang et al. (2018) in their work with block transition probabilities betwen communities.
